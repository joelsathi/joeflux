# Briefing and visual story

## Interview like a director

Read the existing brief, recording notes, and accepted decisions first. Ask one focused question per turn, recommend an answer when evidence supports it, and explain the creative consequence. Do useful asset inspection and rough planning while answers are pending. Ask for missing information, not repeated authorization.

Priority order for a product demo:

1. **Viewer and change:** Who watches, what task frustrates them, and what should they understand or do afterward? Prefer “an IT admin grants access without editing three systems” over “show our powerful platform.”
2. **Proof:** Which exact recorded action and result demonstrate that change? Identify source timestamps. If absent, mark the proof as missing and propose a capture; never invent functionality, speed claims, or success metrics.
3. **Viewing context:** Where will it play, for how long, in which aspect ratio, and at what likely display size? Reuse confirmed output settings. A sales-meeting walkthrough needs different holds from a social teaser.
4. **Taste:** Ask for a reference and the specific moments they like. Offer choices such as “calm and precise” or “fast and graphic,” with consequences for cuts and camera movement. “Premium” alone is not a motion direction.
5. **Constraints:** Brand assets, approved copy, CTA, source quality, localization, delivery deadline, template rights, and the most important failure to avoid.

Follow vague answers with concrete probes:

| User says | Useful next question |
|---|---|
| “Make it dynamic” | “Should the energy come from faster scene changes or from objects moving within a steady scene?” |
| “Like this template” | “Is it the masked text, the camera move, or the connection between shots that you want?” |
| “Show all features” | “Which one workflow would make this viewer want to learn more?” |
| “The animation is basic” | “Which moment loses you: the arrival, the hold, or the handoff to the next shot?” |

After enough information, write back a brief: audience → current problem → one promise → visible proof → desired action; then constraints, chosen direction, assumptions, and unresolved facts. Stop interviewing once the important decisions are clear. If the user delegates style, choose and label a reasoned direction. Unanswered factual claims remain unverified, not assumptions presented as truth.

## Build cause and effect

For a demo, start from **friction → meaningful action → visible result → useful consequence → next step**. Treat this as a useful structure, not a mandatory five-scene template. Show the actual product early enough that viewers know what they are watching. A diagram should explain a relationship the UI cannot show clearly; a title should frame evidence rather than repeat it.

Each beat answers a viewer question and raises the next: “Where is the bottleneck?” → “What changes?” → “Did it work?” A list of unrelated features is not that progression. Preserve important before/after states across shots. Avoid a cut that makes the result appear without its cause.

Draft on-screen copy separately from direction notes. Use concrete verbs and short, accurate claims. For silent demos, copy and visual evidence together must carry the story. Avoid placing a new sentence over a new UI action that also demands reading.

## Example: 30-second access-management demo

This is an illustrative timing sketch, not a claim about a real product:

| Time | Viewer question | Visual evidence | Editorial intention |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0–4s | What is difficult? | Real pending request, context visible | Establish the problem without a long logo opening |
| 4–9s | What do I change? | Relevant policy control in the actual recording | Move from context to one readable detail |
| 9–17s | How does it work? | Action and its observed response | Preserve cause and effect; trim irrelevant waiting |
| 17–25s | What changed? | Verified resulting state | Hold the proof; reduce surrounding movement |
| 25–30s | What next? | One clear CTA and brand | Give the viewer time to read and act |

Adjust those times after inspecting the recording and timing the words. A reference film's pacing is evidence to analyze, not a duration template to copy.
